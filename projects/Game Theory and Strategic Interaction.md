# Game Theory and Strategic Interaction

## Purpose

Build a rigorous, intuitive map of game theory: how multiple agents make decisions when each agent's outcome depends on the choices of others. The project should connect formal models to real situations without losing the assumptions that make each result true.

## Core question

How should we reason about cooperation, competition, coordination, bargaining, and power when the choices of several agents interact?

## Why it matters

Game theory is a common language for strategic situations. It can clarify why individually sensible actions produce bad collective outcomes, when cooperation is stable, how rules change incentives, and where models fail because of information, commitment, repeated interaction, or bounded rationality.

## Scope

### Include

- Basic vocabulary: players, actions, strategies, payoffs, information, beliefs, and solution concepts
- Normal-form and extensive-form games
- Dominance, best responses, Nash equilibrium, mixed strategies, and backward induction
- Repeated, sequential, Bayesian, signaling, bargaining, and coordination games
- Cooperation, credible commitment, reputation, punishment, and social dilemmas
- Mechanism design, auctions, voting, matching, and market design
- Evolutionary game theory and behavioral or boundedly rational approaches
- Applications to organizations, politics, technology, security, and AI alignment/safety
- Canonical examples, formal derivations, empirical evidence, and model limitations

### Exclude for now

- A comprehensive history of every school of thought
- Advanced mathematical proofs before the core concepts are understood
- Treating every optimization or decision problem as a game-theoretic problem
- Public publication before claims, citations, and privacy review are complete

## Learning and research route

1. **Foundations** — formalize games, strategies, payoffs, information, and equilibrium.
2. **Canonical games** — prisoner’s dilemma, coordination, chicken, battle of the sexes, stag hunt, ultimatum, and public-goods games.
3. **Dynamic interaction** — sequential games, repeated games, reputation, threats, promises, and credible commitment.
4. **Private information** — Bayesian games, signaling, screening, adverse selection, and moral hazard.
5. **Designing the rules** — mechanism design, auctions, voting, matching, and institutional incentives.
6. **Beyond ideal rationality** — behavioral, experimental, evolutionary, and learning perspectives.
7. **Applications and synthesis** — connect the framework to organizations, geopolitics, AI systems, and safety problems.

## Working principles

- State the players, available actions, timing, information, and payoffs before interpreting a game.
- Separate a model's prediction from the assumptions that generate it.
- Distinguish Nash equilibrium from desirable, fair, unique, or realistic outcomes.
- Record counterexamples and empirical deviations, not only elegant results.
- Use small payoff matrices and diagrams when they make the mechanism easier to inspect.
- Link distilled ideas to source notes and preserve exact evidence locations for important claims.

## Initial research questions

- What does Nash equilibrium explain, and what does it leave unexplained?
- Under what conditions can cooperation persist in repeated interaction?
- How do information asymmetries change strategic behavior and institutional design?
- When do threats, promises, and commitments become credible?
- How do real agents learn and adapt when they cannot solve a game exactly?
- Which game-theoretic concepts transfer cleanly to multi-agent AI systems, and where does the analogy break?
